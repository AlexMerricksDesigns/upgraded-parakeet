2026 Preface
I wrote the piece below in March 2021, days before the Beeple auction closed. It never made it out of a website development environment — one of several half-built sites that accumulated during a period when the thinking was moving faster than the infrastructure to publish it. Reading it back now, the argument is rougher than I'd have liked but structurally it holds. The paradigm-shift framing survived. The market did not, which is beside the point.
What the subsequent years clarified is that the interesting question was never about NFTs as a technology or as a financial instrument. It was always about the edition — about what it means to fix an instance of a work and attach a provenance record to it, and what that act encodes that posting a JPEG does not. That question is older than blockchain and it is not resolved by it. Photography has been living inside it since the negative.
The copy minting problem that ran through Tezos photography spaces from around 2022 onward sharpened my position considerably — not toward scepticism about the form but toward a more deliberate curatorial practice. If the social agreements underlying a provenance record are fragile, the response is to hold them more carefully, not to abandon them. That is the logic behind Under Orchard, the collecting identity I run within Dérivé Studio, and behind the edition decisions in my own minting work.
What follows the original piece is an account of where that practice has gone: the collected works under Under Orchard across photography, code-based and generative work, and trading card format experiments; and my own photographic editions. The 2021 argument is the foundation. This is what was built on it.

Cryptoart, Value, and a Cultural Paradigm Shift
March 2021, edited for publication 2026

The art world is built on trust and runs on reputation. Galleries have traditionally managed both — communicating confidence, brokering introductions, maintaining the fiction that value is inherent rather than socially constructed. What NFTs do, at their most structurally interesting, is make that fiction visible. The provenance record has always been doing the work. The blockchain just makes it legible.
I am primarily a photographer. The process produces objects at every stage: the RAW file, the edited export, the print, the scan of the negative, the negative itself. The question of which of these is the photograph has no clean answer — only conventions that different contexts weight differently. A signed, numbered edition print commands a premium not because the paper or ink differs materially from an unsigned print of the same image, but because the provenance record encodes a specific set of social agreements about authenticity and scarcity. This is not a property of the object. It is a property of the network of trust surrounding it.
NFTs do not change this structure. They complete it. From the 1/1 physical original through numbered print editions, digital exports, to a 1/1 tokenised digital representation — these are instances along a spectrum, each encoding a different set of agreements about what is being fixed and for whom. The better comparison is not painting versus photograph but unsigned print versus signed edition. What is the qualitative difference between any two prints of a specific edition, or between one edition and another, other than their metadata? The NFT/JPEG distinction is the same distinction. Provenance and metadata, not content.
Trevor Jones, whose practice explicitly straddles physical and digital — minting NFT animations from the same works sold as physical paintings — framed it well: the painting, the digital image, and the animation are connected but independent works. Together they blur the line between material and digital art worlds. I would put it more bluntly: they demonstrate that the line was always permeable, and that value was always distributed across the assemblage rather than residing in any single instance.
The Christie's auction of Beeple's Everydays in March 2021 — running at $9.75m with under twenty-four hours remaining as I first wrote this — was a paradigm-shift moment. Not because it legitimised digital art, which did not need Christie's, but because a major institutional actor staked its reputational infrastructure on the social agreements underlying tokenised provenance. That is the move that matters. Institutions do not just reflect cultural shifts — they ratify them, and in ratifying them, accelerate them.
Thomas Kuhn argued in The Structure of Scientific Revolutions that science does not progress through linear accumulation but through periodic ruptures — paradigm shifts in which the terms of inquiry are transformed rather than extended. The argument generalises: any practice organised around a set of axioms can undergo structural revolution when those axioms shift rather than accumulate. The art world's axioms — around authenticity, scarcity, provenance, institutional authority — are shifting. Christie's putting its name on a JPEG is not an endpoint. It is an inflection point, and inflection points compound.
Where this leads is not yet fully legible. It rarely is from inside the shift. But the structure of the argument is clear enough: the spectrum of the instance is now complete, the provenance record has a new register, and the question of what it means to own, collect, and make within that register is open. That question is what the work below this piece attempts to answer in practice.
